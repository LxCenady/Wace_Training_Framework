# AB · Acids and bases 酸与碱 (Unit 3)

Bank: `CHE/questions.json` (entries whose tags include AB). 49 of 150 bank questions touch AB; AB carries 14.7% of tagged marks. Tags: `tools/parttags_CHE.txt`. Catalogue: `tools/catalogue_CHE.json` (AB1–AB9). Key PDFs: `CHE/papers/<year>_CHE_MarkingKey.pdf`.

## Scope
- Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases, conjugate pairs, amphoteric species.
- Strong and weak acids and bases; ionisation equations (including polyprotic acids in steps); Ka; species present in solution; the Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry models compared.
- Hydrolysis of salts and the pH of salt solutions (including amphoteric anions such as hydrogencarbonate and hydrogensulfite).
- Buffer solutions: composition, equilibrium, Le Chatelier explanation of pH resistance.
- pH, pOH, Kw, pH of strong acid and base solutions, dilution and mixing.
- Indicators, equivalence point, end point, choice of indicator from the pH at equivalence.
- Titration technique: rinsing, primary standards, standardisation, readings, errors.
- Titration curves: shape, equivalence point, strong and weak combinations.
- Non-redox equations and observations for acid–base, precipitation and decomposition reactions.

## How SCSA asks it
- Short items on acid strength and concentration, and on species in solution (AB2, often SA).
- A titration: method or apparatus choice, indicator selection, calculation from the titration results (AB6, AB7, QNT4).
- A buffer: an equation and a Le Chatelier explanation of the pH change after an addition (AB4).
- Observations and equations for a reaction in a mixed-topic question (AB9, 16 bank questions).
- Titration curves are less common (AB8, 3 questions) and usually an ER part.

## Method in brief
- **AB1.** The acid gives H⁺; the conjugate base is the species left after the loss of H⁺, and the two form a conjugate pair. A species that can both give and accept H⁺ (water, HCO₃⁻) is amphoteric; name it as such.
- **AB2.** Strong and weak refers to the degree of ionisation; concentrated and dilute refers to amount per volume. Compare Ka (larger Ka means stronger acid). List all species present, including water, the ions, and the un-ionised acid for a weak acid.
- **AB3.** A salt of a strong acid and strong base is neutral; a weak acid conjugate base gives a basic solution; a weak base conjugate acid gives an acidic solution. Write the hydrolysis equation, then state the pH direction.
- **AB4.** Buffer: a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) in comparable amounts. Add H⁺: the base reacts with it; the equilibrium shifts to reduce the change; pH changes little. Mark the equation and the explanation separately.
- **AB5.** pH = −log[H⁺]; pOH = −log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C (Kw from the data booklet). For a strong base give [OH⁻] first, then [H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻]. Keep pH to the decimal places the data supports.
- **AB6.** The equivalence point is where the moles of acid and base are equal; the end point is the colour change. Choose an indicator whose transition range contains the equivalence pH. Explain the choice with the pH values, not only the names of the indicators.
- **AB7.** Burette and pipette are rinsed with the solution they will hold (distilled water is not enough). A primary standard is pure, stable and of known molar mass. Random error (reading) and systematic error (uncalibrated glassware) are different; state the effect on the calculated concentration.
- **AB9.** Write the ionic equation, then the observations: colour or appearance before mixing, the precipitate or gas formed, and the colour or appearance after. Use the colours from the data booklet.

## Traps (examiners' reports and the catalogue)
- Concentration and strength are different. The examiners' reports (2019–2025 set) note candidates confusing them. Write "a concentrated solution of a weak acid" only when both terms are used correctly.
- A buffer "resists pH change" alone earns little. The explanation must name the equilibrium and the reacting component.
- In a titration, pH at the equivalence point decides the indicator, not the colour the solution reaches first.
- Do not name the chemicals in an observation. Describe colour, precipitate, gas.
- Amphoteric anions (HCO₃⁻, HSO₃⁻): write both the acid and the base equation if asked to compare.
- Rounding: the decimal places of a pH equal the significant figures of the [H⁺] it came from (a 2 s.f. [H⁺] gives pH to 2 decimal places). Check the data before setting the answer precision.

## Generator notes
- Titration questions should give the raw data (volumes, aliquot size, flask volume, concentration of the titrant) in a table, and let the student compute. Check the aliquot-to-flask scaling and the concordant titres.
- A buffer question needs a stated composition (amounts or concentrations of the weak acid and its salt) and an addition of a named strong acid or base.
- AB9 items: one reaction per part, with the observation rows marked separately. Use reactions the syllabus names (precipitation, acid–carbonate, neutralisation).
- For cross-topic work with QNT (the most common pair, 18 bank questions), keep the titration calculation and the equation in one question; keep the observation parts in another.
- Verify: charge and atom balance on each ionic equation; pH values from [H⁺] to the right precision; moles of acid and base at the equivalence point; the chosen indicator's range against the equivalence pH.
